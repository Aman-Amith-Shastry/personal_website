Histopathological Breast Cancer Classification

Michigan Data Science Team · Winter 2025 · main contributor, Vision Transformer

TensorFlowVision TransformerResNetBag of Visual WordsBreakHis

Overview

The publicly available BreakHis biopsy slides carry two labels at once: benign or malignant, and one of eight finer histological subtypes. That setup poses a nice question — if you make a model learn the subtypes first, does it get better at the coarse call that actually matters clinically?

The intuition is that the binary label throws away structure the images plainly contain. A fibroadenoma and a phyllodes tumor are both benign but look nothing alike, and forcing a model to call them the same thing may be asking it to ignore exactly the features that separate them from anything malignant. The alternative is to train on the eight subtypes and map the predictions down to benign or malignant afterwards.

I built and trained the Vision Transformer arm and wrote up the cross-architecture comparison for the team's poster. This was a six-person project under three leads — the other architectures were other members' work.

How it works

Dataset
BreakHis: 9,109 microscopy images of breast tumor tissue in total, of which the 7,909 that are publicly downloadable — 2,480 benign and 5,429 malignant — are what we trained on. Each is labeled both benign or malignant and with one of eight histological subtypes, four of each. Split 80/20.
The comparison
Every architecture is trained twice on the same data: once directly on the benign/malignant label, once on the eight subtypes with the predictions collapsed to binary afterwards. The only thing that varies between the two runs is the label granularity, which is what makes the difference attributable.
Architectures
Four of them, spanning the range from classical to modern: Bag of Visual Words over hand-built local features, a convolutional network trained from scratch, a fine-tuned ResNet, and a Vision Transformer. Running the same experiment across all four is what tells you whether an effect is a property of the labeling or an artifact of one model's inductive bias.
Vision Transformer
My arm. Slides are tiled into patches and attention is computed over the whole image, so unlike the CNN there is no locality prior forcing the model to build up from small neighborhoods — useful on histopathology, where the diagnostic signal is often the global arrangement of tissue rather than any one region of it. Built in TensorFlow.
Result
Learning the subtypes first helped every architecture, which I did not expect to hold so uniformly across four models that fail in such different ways.

What I’d do differently

  • Take the preprocessing as seriously as the models. Augmentation and the train/test split were handled at a baseline level — sensible defaults, not choices we interrogated. Those decisions sit upstream of every architecture we compared, so they deserved their own investigation, along with a tighter control on what was held fixed between the two labeling conditions.
  • Put a confidence interval on the comparison. Training on the subtypes and collapsing to binary came out ahead on every metric we looked at, but those are point estimates from single runs. Bootstrap resampling across models would turn the comparison into something we could actually test — intervals that either separate or overlap — rather than a direction we observed and had to take at face value.
